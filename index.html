How Bitcoin works

Money that no bank or government runs, kept honest by maths and by thousands of computers checking each other. Scroll to see how.

Scroll ↓

Scene 1

The problem: money without a bank

Cash is simple. If Alice hands Bob a banknote, Bob has it and Alice doesn't. It can't be in two places at once.

Digital things aren't like that. A photo, a song, a file: each can be copied perfectly, as many times as you like.

So if money were just a file, Alice could pay Bob and Carol with the very same coin. This is called double spending.

Today, banks prevent this. A bank keeps a ledger, a record of who owns what, and refuses any payment that spends the same money twice.

That works, but everyone has to trust the bank. Bitcoin's idea: give everyone a copy of the ledger, and a way to agree on it, so that no one needs to be in charge.

Scene 2

Wallets and keys

To use Bitcoin you don't open an account. Your wallet app simply picks an enormous random number. That number is your private key.

From the private key, maths produces a public key, and from that an address. It only works in one direction: nobody can run it backwards.

The address is like an account number. Give it to anyone who wants to pay you.

The private key is the only pen that can sign for your coins. Anyone who gets hold of it can spend them, and there is no "forgot my password".

So a wallet doesn't really hold coins. It holds keys. The coins are entries on the shared ledger, and the keys prove which ones are yours to spend.

Scene 3

A transaction: sending coins

Alice wants to send Bob 0.3 bitcoin. Her wallet holds one coin worth 0.5.

She writes a transaction: a short public message saying which coin she is spending and where the value should go.

She signs it with her private key. The key never leaves her wallet. Only the signature is attached.

Anyone can check that signature using Alice's public key. It proves the coin's owner approved exactly this message, and nothing else.

A coin is always spent whole. 0.3 goes to Bob, 0.19 comes back to Alice as change, and 0.01 is left as a fee for the miner who confirms it.

Nothing is final yet. The transaction is broadcast to the network, where it waits to be confirmed. Until then, the payment is only pending.

Scene 4

The mempool: the waiting room

Alice's transaction passes from computer to computer, and within seconds most of the network has seen it.

Each computer checks it first. Is the signature valid? Has this coin been spent already? If it passes, it joins that computer's waiting area: the mempool.

It isn't alone. Thousands of other transactions are waiting too, each offering a fee.

Space in the next block is limited, so miners usually pick the transactions that pay the most. A low fee can mean a long wait.

Scene 5

Hashing: digital fingerprints

Before going further, we need one tool. A hash function takes any text and turns it into a fixed-length fingerprint, called a hash. Bitcoin uses one named SHA-256.

The same text always gives the same fingerprint. Every time, on every computer.

Change a single letter and the fingerprint changes completely, in a way no one can predict.

And it only goes one way. Given a fingerprint, there is no way to work out the text that made it, other than guessing.

Scene 6

Blocks and the chain

About every ten minutes, a batch of waiting transactions is bundled together into a block.

Each block gets a fingerprint: the hash of everything inside it.

Here is the clever part. Every block also records the fingerprint of the block before it. That links them, one after another, into a chain: the blockchain.

Now suppose someone quietly edits an old transaction. That block's contents change, so its fingerprint changes too.

The next block still records the old fingerprint, so the link snaps. Fixing it would change that block's fingerprint as well, breaking the one after it, and so on down the chain. The tampering is obvious to everyone.

Scene 7

Mining and proof of work

Bitcoin makes blocks hard to create, on purpose. A block only counts if its fingerprint starts with a required run of zeros.

Fingerprints can't be steered, so the only way is trial and error. Miners add a throwaway number, the nonce, hash the block, and look. Wrong? Change the nonce and try again. Every guess costs electricity.

Miners around the world make hundreds of quintillions of guesses every second. Whoever hits a valid fingerprint first adds the block and earns a reward. The difficulty adjusts so this takes about ten minutes.

Checking the winner takes a single hash. Hard to find, instant to verify: that is proof of work.

This is what protects the chain. To rewrite an old block, a cheat must redo its work and the work of every block since, while the rest of the world keeps adding new ones. They fall further and further behind.

Scene 8

The network and consensus

Bitcoin runs on tens of thousands of computers around the world, called nodes. Each keeps its own complete copy of the blockchain.

When a miner finds a block, it passes it to its neighbours. Each node checks every rule for itself before passing it on. Nobody takes anybody's word for anything.

Sometimes two miners find a block at almost the same moment, and for a while the network holds two versions of the latest block.

The tie breaks itself. Whichever version gets the next block built on top of it has more work behind it, and every node switches to that chain. In practice that is the longest one.

This is why people wait for confirmations. Every block added on top of yours makes your payment harder to undo. Six is the traditional rule of thumb for large amounts.

Scene 9

Supply: 21 million, and no more

Where do bitcoins come from? The miner who wins each block is allowed to create a fixed number of brand-new coins: the block reward. It is the only way bitcoins are made.

In 2009 the reward was 50 bitcoin. Every 210,000 blocks, roughly four years, it is cut in half. Each cut is called a halving.

Since the 2024 halving, each block creates 3.125 new bitcoin. The next one, expected in 2028, will take that down to 1.5625.

Keep halving and the total can never pass a ceiling. For Bitcoin, that ceiling is just under 21 million coins. About 95% of them already exist.

The last new coins are expected around the year 2140. After that, miners will be paid by transaction fees alone. Nobody can print extra: every node checks the reward in every block.

Recap

The whole journey

Sign. Alice writes a transaction and signs it with her private key.

Wait. It spreads across the network and sits in the mempool.

Mine. A miner bundles it into a block and spends real energy finding a valid hash.

Link. The block records the hash of the one before it, extending the chain.

Agree. Every node checks the block and adds it to its own copy. No bank and no one in charge: just rules that anyone can verify.
