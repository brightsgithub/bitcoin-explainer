Scene 1

The problem: money without a bank

Cash is simple. If Alice hands Bob a banknote, Bob has it and Alice doesn't. It can't be in two places at once.

Digital things aren't like that. A photo, a song, a file: each can be copied perfectly, as many times as you like.

So if money were just a file, Alice could pay Bob and Carol with the very same coin. This is called double spending.

Today, banks prevent this. A bank keeps a ledger, a record of who owns what, and refuses any payment that spends the same money twice.

That works, but everyone has to trust the bank. Bitcoin's idea: give everyone a copy of the ledger, and a way to agree on it, so that no one needs to be in charge.

Scene 3

A transaction: sending coins

Alice wants to send Bob 0.3 bitcoin. Her wallet holds one coin worth 0.5.

She writes a transaction: a short public message saying which coin she is spending and where the value should go.

She signs it with her private key. The key never leaves her wallet. Only the signature is attached.

Anyone can check that signature using Alice's public key. It proves the coin's owner approved exactly this message, and nothing else.

A coin is always spent whole. 0.3 goes to Bob, 0.19 comes back to Alice as change, and 0.01 is left as a fee for the miner who confirms it.

Nothing is final yet. The transaction is broadcast to the network, where it waits to be confirmed. Until then, the payment is only pending.
